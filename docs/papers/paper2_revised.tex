% ABSTRACT
% ==========================================
\begin{abstract}
Automated engagement monitoring systems frequently encounter a structural ambiguity when applied to classroom environments. Facial expressions commonly associated with negative affect—such as furrowed brows or sustained gaze—also appear during periods of intense cognitive concentration. As instructional complexity increases, unimodal vision-based models therefore risk systematically misinterpreting focused effort as disengagement. Conventional cost-sensitive learning approaches attempt to compensate for this asymmetry during training, yet the reasoning behind individual predictions often remains difficult to interpret.

To address this ambiguity, this study proposes a context-aware fusion layer that explicitly models the imbalance between observed facial valence and instructional context. The framework integrates facial valence estimation with schedule metadata and real-time lexical density extracted from instructional speech. During segments characterized by high instructional load, the fusion logic adaptively adjusts the engagement decision boundary. We describe this recurring misinterpretation as a ``Valence Discrepancy'' and treat lexical density as a context-conditioned Bayesian prior.

Across randomized splits of the Sim-Class-24 staged dataset, the proposed framework reduces the Simulation False Negative Rate under High Load (Sim-FNR-HL) to 6.0\% (95\% CI [4.1\%, 7.9\%]), compared with 42.0\% for a vision-only baseline. Instead of relying exclusively on opaque deep fusion architectures, the framework emphasizes structured and auditable risk control. These results suggest that contextual priors grounded in instructional semantics can substantially reduce asymmetric classification errors while preserving interpretability in pedagogical analytics frameworks.
\end{abstract}

% ==========================================
% I. INTRODUCTION
% ==========================================
\section{Introduction}

A persistent challenge in learning analytics is distinguishing disengagement from concentrated cognitive effort. Visual indicators such as facial tension, reduced expressivity, and sustained gaze are often interpreted as negative affect by standard affective computing models. However, within academic environments—particularly during technically demanding instruction—these same signals frequently reflect active cognitive processing rather than disengagement. The difficulty stems from a mismatch between low-level facial cues—muscle movements, gaze patterns—and the higher-level cognitive states they are assumed to represent.

This mismatch creates a structural interpretation problem for unimodal engagement classifiers. Standard affective computing models, trained on emotion recognition datasets such as FER-2013 or CK+, tend to associate these cues with negative affect. Yet in academic settings, the same expressions often reflect sustained cognitive effort rather than boredom or disengagement \cite{b4, b5, b20}.

The resulting ambiguity produces a measurable statistical bias. As instructional complexity increases, unimodal vision systems become progressively more likely to misclassify focused concentration as disengagement. The system can inadvertently penalize productive struggle—the very condition required for learning progression.

This study therefore treats engagement as a context-conditioned attentional proxy rather than a direct emotional state. Our objective is not emotional labeling, but preventing systematic misinterpretation during high cognitive load.

Importantly, engagement monitoring exhibits asymmetric classification costs. A Type-II error—labeling an engaged student as disengaged—can generate unnecessary alerts during critical learning moments. Repeated false alerts can erode instructor trust and undermine system adoption. In contrast, occasional Type-I errors (failing to detect minor distraction) are generally less disruptive.

Most end-to-end deep learning models internalize this trade-off implicitly within the loss function. Here, we instead make the asymmetry explicit. The proposed fusion framework makes error priorities explicit through structured priors and temporal smoothing, allowing instructors to audit how contextual signals influence decisions.

This study isolates the logical and mathematical formulation of the fusion layer, independent of identity resolution or distributed deployment concerns.

% II. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
% ==========================================
\section{Theoretical Framework and Contextual Modeling}

\subsection{Limitations of Unimodal Valence Estimation}
Early engagement monitoring systems relied heavily on facial expression recognition (FER), mapping facial landmarks to predefined emotional categories \cite{b2}. While effective in controlled affective computing settings, such mappings assume that emotional valence directly corresponds to behavioral engagement.

In educational contexts, this assumption does not consistently hold. Prior research has shown that pedagogical engagement occupies a multi-dimensional space distinct from raw emotional valence \cite{b7}. A student exhibiting neutral-to-negative facial expressions may still be deeply attentive. Unimodal pipelines typically lack the contextual grounding necessary to differentiate between frustration that impedes learning and confusion or concentration that facilitates it.

Without environmental conditioning, visual models may therefore be prone to over-interpreting negative facial signals.

\subsection{Formalizing the Valence Discrepancy}
To capture this systematic misinterpretation, we define the Valence Discrepancy ($D_{valence}$) as:
\begin{equation}
D_{valence} = P(V_{neg} \mid HighLoad)
\end{equation}
Here:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_{neg}$ denotes detected negative visual valence.
    \item $HighLoad$ represents periods of elevated instructional complexity.
\end{itemize}

If $D_{valence} > \tau$, where $\tau$ reflects baseline negativity derived from neutral task distributions, a unimodal classifier will consistently mislabel concentrated effort as disengagement. The greater the instructional intensity, the higher the probability of systematic error.

This definition reframes the problem as a conditional probability issue rather than simple feature misclassification. The fusion logic therefore benefits from conditioning on environmental state variables to remain reliable.

\subsection{Cognitive Load Theory and Affective States}

Cognitive Load Theory provides the pedagogical grounding for this conditioning \cite{b5}. Working memory is limited, and meaningful learning requires germane cognitive load—the mental effort devoted to constructing and refining knowledge structures.

During periods of high germane load, observable behavior can shift. Micro-expressions diminish, facial tension increases, and socially expressive behaviors such as smiling often decrease. A purely affect-driven classifier may interpret these signals as negative affect, even though they correlate with productive cognitive processing.

By incorporating schedule metadata and semantic density extracted from instructional speech, the proposed framework adjusts its interpretation accordingly \cite{b10}. Rather than suppressing visual signals, it recontextualizes them.

Negative valence is not ignored; it is evaluated relative to environmental load.

\subsection{Formalizing the Risk Function}
In institutional environments, not all classification errors carry equal weight. Let:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $y \in \{engaged, disengaged\}$
    \item $\hat{y}$ be the predicted state
\end{itemize}

We explicitly encode the relationship:
\begin{equation}
C_{FN} \gg C_{FP}
\end{equation}
reflecting that false negatives (mislabeling engaged students as disengaged) are more disruptive than false positives \cite{b18}.

The expected risk $R$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
R = C_{FP} P(\hat{y}=E \mid y=D) + C_{FN} P(\hat{y}=D \mid y=E)
\end{equation}

Standard classifiers implicitly assume $C_{FP} = C_{FN}$. The proposed approach introduces contextual modulation during identified high-load intervals, structurally reducing:
\begin{equation}
P(\hat{y}=D \mid y=E)
\end{equation}
This re-weighting aligns logic behavior with institutional priorities rather than pure accuracy optimization.

\subsection{Bayesian Prior Shift Interpretation}

The contextual logic layer can be interpreted as a prior adjustment within a Bayesian decision framework.

Rather than retraining the visual model or modifying its weights, we adjust the effective prior probability of engagement during high-load periods. As instructional lexical density increases, the prior probability of engagement rises proportionally \cite{b19}.

The engagement posterior is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
P(E \mid V, C_{load}) = \frac{P(V \mid E) \cdot P(E \mid C_{load})}{P(V \mid C_{load})}
\end{equation}

In practice, this means stronger visual evidence is required before disengagement is inferred during dense instructional segments. The logic shifts expectation rather than altering the underlying visual evidence.

\subsection{Derivation of the Re-weighting Equation}
Audio semantic density $C_{load}(t)$ is computed over a rolling window $\Delta t$:
\begin{equation}
C_{load}(t) = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \int_{t-\Delta t}^{t} \frac{\sum_{w \in W_{domain}} \mathbb{I}(speech(w))}{\sum w_{total}} d\tau
\end{equation}
This captures the proportion of domain-specific instructional tokens relative to total speech.

The final engagement score is computed as:
\begin{equation}
E_{score}(t) = \alpha \cdot (1 - V_{neg}) + \beta \cdot \left[ \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k(C_{load}(t) - \mu)}} \right]
\end{equation}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha + \beta = 1$
    \item $\beta \leq 0.7$ (to prevent overcorrection)
    \item $\mu$ defines the load transition threshold
    \item $k$ controls transition sharpness
\end{itemize}

The sigmoid ensures smooth mode transitions rather than abrupt threshold flips.

\subsection{Temporal Hysteresis}
Rapid fluctuations in speech or facial micro-movements can cause unstable outputs. To mitigate flickering, a first-order IIR filter is applied \cite{b16}:
\begin{equation}
E_{smooth}(t) = \gamma E_{score}(t) + (1-\gamma) E_{smooth}(t-1)
\end{equation}
With $\gamma = 0.2$, the effective smoothing window approximates five seconds. This stabilizes output while preserving responsiveness.

% IV. PIPELINE ARCHITECTURE & ALGORITHMS
% ==========================================
\section{Pipeline Architecture and Algorithms}
To operationalize this contextual conditioning, the proposed framework processes visual, acoustic, and schedule signals through a modular multi-stream pipeline. Visual and acoustic streams are processed independently before fusion occurs in the logic layer.

This separation ensures that delays in semantic processing do not block the high-frequency visual inference loop.

\subsection{Acoustic Extraction and Voice Activity Detection}
Continuous semantic processing during silence introduces unnecessary latency. A lightweight Voice Activity Detector (VAD) evaluates root-mean-square energy:
\begin{equation}
E_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N B[i]^2}
\end{equation}
Frames below a -40 dB threshold are filtered out. ASR inference is triggered only when VAD probability exceeds 0.85 for a sustained duration \cite{b13, b22}.

Importantly, ASR runs in an asynchronous background thread. The visual pipeline continues uninterrupted, preserving real-time performance. Domain-specific dictionaries $\mathcal{D}_{STEM}$, injected through schedule metadata, guide semantic density estimation.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Semantic Density Extraction Pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Audio Buffer $B$, Schedule Subject $S$, Dictionary $\mathcal{D}$
\ENSURE Context Load $C_{load}$
\STATE $E_{rms} \leftarrow \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N B[i]^2}$
\IF{$E_{rms} < \text{Threshold}_{VAD}$}
    \RETURN $0.0$ \COMMENT{Silence, no load update}
\ENDIF
\STATE $Tokens \leftarrow \text{ASR\_Inference}(B)$
\STATE $Count_{domain} \leftarrow 0$
\FOR{each $w$ in $Tokens$}
    \IF{$w \in \mathcal{D}[S]$}
        \STATE $Count_{domain} \leftarrow Count_{domain} + 1$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE $C_{load} \leftarrow \min(1.0, \frac{Count_{domain}}{|Tokens| \times \text{ScaleFactor}})$
\RETURN $C_{load}$
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:audio}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Visual Pre-processing and Valence Extraction}
Lecture halls present variable lighting conditions. Raw RGB frames often produce unstable valence predictions. We apply Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to enhance local contrast without amplifying global noise.

This preprocessing stabilizes extraction of the $V_{neg}$ vector under projector glare, dimmed lighting, and partial occlusion.

\subsection{Context-Aware Re-weighting Logic}
The final fusion stage conditionally applies the Bayesian prior shift.

If:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Subject type = STEM
    \item $V_{neg} < \tau_{neg}$
\end{itemize}
Then contextual boosting is applied via the sigmoid prior adjustment. Otherwise, standard visual evaluation proceeds unmodified.

The smoothed engagement score is updated iteratively, preserving temporal continuity. This layered structure makes the decision path inspectable. Each component—visual evidence, contextual density, schedule metadata—remains independently traceable.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Context-Aware Affect Re-weighting}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE VisualVector $V$, Density $C_{load}$, Schedule $S$
\ENSURE Smoothed Engagement Score $E_{smooth}$
\STATE $Subj \leftarrow S.get\_metadata(t_{now})$
\STATE $V_{vec} \leftarrow \text{Classify\_Valence}(V)$
\IF{$Subj.type == \text{STEM}$ \textbf{and} $V_{vec}.valence < \tau_{neg}$}
    \STATE \textbf{// Apply Bayesian Prior Shift via Sigmoid}
    \STATE $Boost \leftarrow \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-k(C_{load} - \mu))}$
    \STATE $E \leftarrow \alpha \cdot (1.0 - V_{vec}.valence) + \beta \cdot Boost$
\ELSE
    \STATE \textbf{// Standard unmodulated evaluation}
    \STATE $E \leftarrow (1.0 - V_{vec}.valence)$
\ENDIF
\STATE $E_{smooth} \leftarrow \gamma E + (1-\gamma) E_{prev}$
\STATE $E_{prev} \leftarrow E_{smooth}$
\RETURN $E_{smooth}$
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:context}
\end{algorithm}

% V. SIMULATION DESIGN (SIM-CLASS-24)

\section{Simulation Design (Sim-Class-24)}

Empirical validation requires controlled observation of the engagement ambiguity described above. To evaluate the fusion logic without exposing real student data during early prototyping, we constructed a controlled dataset referred to as Sim-Class-24.

\subsection{Simulation Protocol}
The dataset consists of 24 staged classroom sessions, each lasting approximately 60 minutes, yielding $N = 1,440$ evaluated temporal windows. Rather than collecting naturalistic classroom recordings at this stage, we scripted controlled behavioral conditions to isolate the logic layer under reproducible circumstances.

Three scenario types were defined:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline (Scenario A):} Non-technical discussion with low lexical density and neutral-to-positive facial expressions.
    \item \textbf{High Load (Scenario B):} Simulated deep technical instruction. Actors maintained visible concentration markers (e.g., furrowed brows, minimal facial movement), while the audio stream included dense STEM terminology such as “derivative,” “matrix,” and “theorem.”
    \item \textbf{Mixed (Scenario C):} Alternating phases of listening, note-taking (including partial occlusion), and mild distraction.
\end{enumerate}

During early pilot recordings, we observed exaggerated facial tension during simulated “concentration.” To prevent unrealistic valence inflation, we conducted rehearsal calibration sessions to constrain expressions within ranges observed in real classroom environments.

Ground truth labels were determined according to scripted engagement states. Five-fold cross-validation was applied across randomized splits of the sessions. The goal of this dataset is not immediate ecological generalization, but controlled validation of the fusion hypothesis.

\subsection{Noise Injection and Environmental Variability}
To avoid overfitting to ideal recording conditions, environmental perturbations were introduced synthetically:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Occlusion:} Randomized bounding box drops simulated head turns and hand-to-face gestures.
    \item \textbf{Lighting variability:} Gamma correction ($\gamma \in [0.5, 1.5]$) and additive Gaussian noise ($\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$) simulated projector glare and dim lecture halls.
\end{itemize}

These perturbations ensured that improvements were unlikely to be artifacts of pristine data.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison and Statistical Significance}
We compared the proposed framework against:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Vision-Only Baseline
    \item Weighted Cross-Entropy ResNet (WCE-ResNet) \cite{b26}
    \item Cost-Sensitive Multimodal Transformer (CS-Transformer) \cite{b21}
    \item Prosodic Multimodal Fusion Model
\end{enumerate}

To ensure fairness, model sizes were constrained to similar parameter counts ($\approx 80M$) to avoid latency bias.

The key metric, Simulation False Negative Rate under High Load (Sim-FNR-HL), was reduced from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 42.0\% (Vision Only)
\end{itemize}
to
\begin{itemize}
    \item 6.0\% (Proposed Logic Layer)
\end{itemize}

The 95\% confidence interval [4.1\%, 7.9\%] indicates statistical robustness across cross-validation folds. A paired t-test confirms significant reduction in Type-II errors ($N = 1,440$, $p < 0.001$).

While false positives increase modestly during borderline low-load discussion, instructor feedback during pilot review indicated that preventing false disengagement alerts during productive struggle was the higher priority. This trade-off is intentional and explicitly encoded in the asymmetric risk formulation.

\section{Discussion}

Taken together, the results illustrate how contextual signals reshape the interpretation of visual affect during instructional activity. 

\subsection{Comparison with Prosodic Fusion Approaches}
Many multimodal systems emphasize prosodic signals such as pitch variation and speech rate \cite{b9}. While useful in conversational contexts, these cues may remain relatively flat during technically dense lectures delivered in a steady tone.

Lexical density, in contrast, tends to reflect semantic complexity more directly. In academic settings where instruction involves domain-specific terminology, lexical cues align more closely with cognitive load than prosodic fluctuations. This structural alignment explains the measurable improvement over standard prosodic fusion systems.

\subsection{Explainability and Pedagogical Transparency}
End-to-end multimodal Transformers optimize classification objectives but obscure the rationale behind individual predictions \cite{b11}. In educational systems, interpretability is particularly important.

The proposed logic layer encodes pedagogical assumptions explicitly. Each alert can be traced to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Visual valence score
    \item Acoustic density value
    \item Schedule metadata
\end{itemize}

This traceability supports instructor trust and aligns with principles of Explainable AI (XAI) \cite{b23}. Rather than hiding asymmetry within model weights, we encode it in transparent logic.

% ==========================================
% VIII. LIMITATIONS
% ==========================================
\section{Limitations}
All quantitative results are derived from the scripted Sim-Class-24 dataset. Although environmental noise was injected, real classrooms introduce additional variability not captured in simulation.

The semantic density metric assumes active verbal instruction. During silent reading or written assessments, $C_{load}$ defaults toward zero, reducing the framework to its visual baseline.

Additionally, lexical density depends on domain-specific keywords. Conceptually dense but lexically simple explanations may not trigger the high-load prior. Field validation across diverse classroom environments remains necessary before deployment.

% ==========================================
% IX. CONCLUSION
% ==========================================
\section{Conclusion}
This study examined a structural ambiguity in engagement analytics: the tendency of unimodal vision systems to misinterpret concentrated cognitive effort as disengagement. By framing the problem as asymmetric risk minimization and incorporating semantic acoustic priors alongside schedule metadata, the framework reduces the Simulation False Negative Rate under High Load from 42.0\% to approximately 6.0\% within the simulated evaluation environment.

These improvements were statistically significant relative to vision-only and cost-sensitive deep learning baselines under controlled conditions. Importantly, the contribution lies less in deeper model architectures and more in explicit contextual risk modeling.

The results suggest that relatively lightweight fusion layers, when grounded in pedagogical theory and probabilistic reasoning, can meaningfully shift the error balance while preserving interpretability. Context-conditioned priors therefore provide a practical mechanism for aligning engagement analytics with pedagogical realities while preserving model transparency.
