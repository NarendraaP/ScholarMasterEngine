% ABSTRACT
% ==========================================
\begin{abstract}
Institutional safety monitoring relies heavily on visual surveillance, yet many indoor environments contain regions that cameras cannot observe effectively. Corridor bends, architectural junctions, and partially occluded spaces frequently create blind zones in which optical perception cannot provide timely detection. Acoustic sensing offers a complementary physical modality: sound propagates omnidirectionally and can reflect or diffract beyond visual boundaries. However, practical acoustic deployment must balance two competing requirements—rapid anomaly detection and strict limits on persistent audio retention.

To address this limitation, the present study develops an acoustic sensing module capable of detecting high-decibel impulsive events originating from visually occluded regions. Incoming audio is processed within a bounded circular RAM buffer secured against swap persistence. Spectral energy and periodicity features are extracted locally, while raw samples are overwritten immediately following analysis.

A lightweight Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)–based spectral gate isolates broadband impulse signatures, while an autocorrelation-derived periodic rejection metric suppresses recurring mechanical artifacts. For localization within reverberant corridor geometries, Generalized Cross-Correlation with Phase Transform (GCC-PHAT) is applied under a formally defined reverberant-field operating regime.

Under controlled corridor evaluation, detection probability exceeded 95\% within a 15-meter safety envelope, with end-to-end latency remaining below approximately 120 ms. These findings suggest that physics-driven acoustic sensing can extend spatial awareness into non-line-of-sight spaces while preserving strict constraints on latency and audio data retention.
\end{abstract}

% I. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Non-Line-of-Sight Problem}
Visual perception within indoor environments frequently depends on camera-based surveillance. While modern video systems provide high spatial resolution, their effectiveness is fundamentally constrained by line-of-sight geometry. Architectural features such as corridor bends, stairwell junctions, and structural columns routinely create regions that remain visually unobserved.

These blind regions introduce a practical sensing challenge. In impulsive events, these blind regions are not edge cases; they are precisely where detection delays can matter most.

Acoustic sensing provides an alternative physical modality that does not rely on direct optical visibility. Sound propagates omnidirectionally and is capable of diffracting around physical obstacles. In enclosed corridors, reflections from rigid surfaces extend acoustic reach well beyond visual coverage. Rather than replacing cameras, acoustic sensing can serve as a sentinel layer in regions where geometry restricts optical visibility.

The practical question becomes whether acoustic sensing can operate with bounded latency and without accumulating sensitive audio data.

\subsection{Operational Constraints in Reverberant Corridors}
Deploying acoustic sensing within indoor corridors introduces several practical constraints. Long and narrow geometries constructed with painted concrete, vinyl tile, and rigid ceilings generate strong multipath reflections. In such settings, the reverberant field often dominates direct-path energy beyond a few meters \cite{b25}.

Simple amplitude triggers often perform poorly in these conditions. Volume spikes from mechanical systems, dropped objects, or transient impacts routinely exceed fixed thresholds. Standard Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) localization degrades as delayed echoes obscure the earliest arrival.

At the same time, deep-learning-based Acoustic Event Detection (AED) models introduce their own limitations. Architectures such as Audio Spectrogram Transformers (AST) require multi-second context windows and significant memory allocation. In safety-critical scenarios, buffering multi-second audio segments before inference is generally incompatible with sub-150 ms response requirements \cite{b26}.

The acoustic sensing module presented here adopts a different stance. Rather than semantic classification, it performs localized physical signal discrimination within a circular RAM buffer secured via abstract memory-pinning. Only derived features—energy ratios, periodicity metrics, azimuth estimates—are retained. Raw PCM segments are overwritten immediately after evaluation.

The processing approach is intentionally limited: low latency, bounded memory, no semantic retention.

\subsection{Contributions}
This work operationalizes acoustic sensing under explicit latency and privacy constraints. This work does not employ machine learning or adaptive models. All detection is based on deterministic signal processing. The principal elements include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A formal definition of a reverberant-field operating regime, $\mathcal{R}_{rev}$, aligned with corridor geometries where the safety envelope substantially exceeds the acoustic critical distance.
    \item A two-stage filtering architecture combining spectral discrimination ($\delta$) and periodicity rejection ($\rho$) to reduce mechanical false positives.
    \item A latency-bounded GCC-PHAT implementation for azimuth estimation under multipath-dominant conditions.
    \item Empirical validation across staged and RIR-convolved corridor datasets demonstrating high detection rates within a 15 m non-line-of-sight radius.
\end{itemize}

The detection model does not attempt semantic audio interpretation. Its scope is deliberately narrower: detect high-energy, chaotic impulses and estimate their direction quickly.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Deep Learning AED vs. Edge Signal Processing}
Most recent acoustic event detection systems rely on deep neural architectures trained for semantic sound classification \cite{b8, b12}. Architectures such as PANNs \cite{b22} and AST \cite{b23} achieve strong performance on benchmark datasets including FSD50K. These systems operate on mel-spectrogram patches, often spanning several seconds.

Longer temporal context windows can improve semantic recognition accuracy but introduces structural latency. Buffering 10-second windows before inference inherently violates strict response constraints. Additionally, large transformer-based models (e.g., $\sim$85M parameters) impose heavy memory and bandwidth demands \cite{b15, b16}, especially on ARM-based edge hardware lacking dedicated NPUs.

In safety contexts, the objective differs from environmental classification. The goal is rapid detection of high-energy anomalies. \subsection{Source Localization Under Reverberation}
Source localization is fundamentally governed by the physics of acoustic wave propagation \cite{b9}. Microphone array approaches have demonstrated effective detection of screams and gunshot events \cite{b5}, but indoor corridors introduce severe reverberation \cite{b25}.

Steered Response Power with PHAT weighting (SRP-PHAT) \cite{b27} achieves high localization accuracy but requires scanning large spatial grids, increasing computational load beyond what lightweight edge nodes comfortably sustain.

Standard cross-correlation TDOA methods, though inexpensive, degrade rapidly when multipath reflections dominate. Echoes introduce multiple delayed peaks, obscuring the true time offset.

GCC-PHAT \cite{b20} offers a compromise. By normalizing the cross-spectrum magnitude, it emphasizes phase alignment and mitigates reverberant smearing while retaining manageable computational complexity. This balance makes it practical for bounded-latency edge deployment.

% III. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
% ==========================================
\section{Theoretical Framework}

To analyze the feasibility of acoustic monitoring in visually occluded corridors, we begin by examining the physical propagation characteristics of sound in such environments.

\subsection{Corridor Acoustics and SPL Calibration}
Sound propagation in long corridors deviates substantially from free-field assumptions. The classical inverse-square decay relationship applies primarily when direct-path energy dominates and reflections are negligible. In institutional hallways with hard, reflective surfaces, multipath reinforcement alters this behavior.

The Sound Pressure Level (SPL) at distance $r$ from an omnidirectional source of sound power $L_W$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
L_p(r) = L_W + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi r^2} + \frac{4}{R_c}\right)
\end{equation}
where $Q$ denotes the source directivity factor and $R_c$ is the room constant.

In practical terms, a human scream often produces approximately 95–105 dB at a one-meter distance \cite{b5}. Glass fracture events frequently exceed 100 dB. Using representative corridor absorption coefficients ($\alpha \approx 0.05–0.1$) and measured geometry, a 100 dB impulse generated in a blind segment registers approximately 74 dB at a sensor positioned 20 meters away.

Rather than triggering on raw received amplitude, the signal-processing pipeline calibrates its primary threshold relative to an equivalent 95 dB source level mapped onto a defined safety envelope:
\begin{equation}
S_{env} = 15m
\end{equation}
This mapping suppresses distant, irrelevant disturbances while preserving sensitivity to high-energy events within the operational radius.

\subsection{Reverberation and the Operating Regime}
In many institutional corridors, reverberation becomes the dominant acoustic phenomenon beyond a short distance from the source. The transition between direct-field and reverberant-field dominance occurs at the critical distance $D_c$, defined as the point where direct and reverberant energies are equal.

Using Sabine’s formula, the reverberation time $RT_{60}$ is:
\begin{equation}
RT_{60} = \frac{0.161 V}{A_{eq}}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the corridor volume and $A_{eq}$ the equivalent absorption area. The critical distance follows:
\begin{equation}
D_c \approx 0.057 \sqrt{\frac{V}{RT_{60}}}
\end{equation}

In measured institutional corridors (vinyl tile flooring, painted concrete walls), $RT_{60}$ commonly exceeds 1.2 seconds. Under such conditions, $D_c$ is typically below 3 meters.

The operational regime is therefore explicitly defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{rev} = \{ r : r > D_c \}
\end{equation}
This is not a near-field voice assistant environment. The detection model is tuned for conditions where the reverberant field dominates and the safety envelope satisfies:
\begin{equation}
S_{env} \gg D_c
\end{equation}
By formalizing this regime, the design avoids assumptions of direct-path dominance and instead builds robustness into multipath-heavy conditions.

\subsection{Autocorrelation-Based Periodic Rejection}
High-amplitude triggers alone are insufficient for reliable anomaly detection in reverberant environments. Mechanical infrastructure frequently produces energetic but structurally periodic waveforms.

To discriminate chaotic impulses from periodic artifacts, we compute the discrete autocorrelation function:
\begin{equation}
R_{xx}(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x(n)x(n+k)
\end{equation}
From this, we define the periodicity ratio:
\begin{equation}
\rho = \frac{\max_{k > k_{min}} R_{xx}(k)}{R_{xx}(0)}
\end{equation}
Here, $k_{min}$ excludes the zero-lag peak and is chosen based on the highest expected fundamental mechanical frequency ($f_0 \approx 120Hz$).

Impulsive safety events generally produce rapidly decaying autocorrelation structures. Their $\rho$ values remain well below the empirically determined threshold:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{thresh} = 0.65
\end{equation}
Mechanical signals, by contrast, typically exhibit repeated peaks and elevated $\rho$. During early deployment tests, HVAC startup transients occasionally bypassed the spectral gate due to broadband onset energy. Increasing $k_{min}$ mitigated this issue by excluding short-lag reinforcement patterns associated with mechanical cycling. This adjustment was empirical rather than theoretical, reflecting corridor-specific acoustics.

\subsection{GCC-PHAT for Multipath Mitigation}

Estimating source direction under strong multipath conditions presents an additional challenge. Within $\mathcal{R}_{rev}$, naive cross-correlation methods often fail to isolate the true time delay because reflections create multiple high-energy peaks. The Generalized Cross-Correlation framework addresses this limitation by operating in the frequency domain.

The Cross-Power Spectral Density between two microphone signals is:
\begin{equation}
G_{12}(f) = X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f)
\end{equation}
To suppress magnitude dominance from reverberant energy, PHAT weighting is applied:
\begin{equation}
R_{phat}(f) = \frac{G_{12}(f)}{|G_{12}(f)|} = e^{j\angle G_{12}(f)}
\end{equation}
This normalization effectively whitens the spectrum. In the ideal case, the inverse Fourier transform of $e^{j\angle G_{12}(f)}$ ideally produces a Dirac impulse $\delta(t - \tau)$ at the true delay $\tau$.

In practice, the equalization increases robustness to multipath but does not eliminate all distortion. Under extreme narrowband interference, PHAT weighting can amplify spectral irregularities and broaden the peak. In metallic corridor segments, azimuth deviations of approximately $\pm 8^\circ$ were occasionally observed. Pre-bandpass filtering in the 2–4 kHz impulse band mitigated this effect and sharpened the delay peak. The approach therefore balances theoretical robustness with corridor-specific tuning.

\section{System Implementation}

The proposed acoustic sensing module was implemented as a lightweight pipeline designed for deterministic latency.

\subsection{Hardware Architecture and Sensor Geometry}
The signal-processing algorithms were evaluated on a commodity ARM Cortex-A72 hardware platform equipped with a linear four-microphone array. Audio sampling was performed at 44.1 kHz with 16-bit resolution—sufficient to capture broadband impulsive signatures without excessive computational overhead.

Microphone spacing directly influences spatial aliasing constraints. The spatial aliasing frequency limit is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f_{alias} = \frac{c}{2d \sin(\theta_{max})}
\end{equation}
where $c \approx 343$ m/s denotes the speed of sound, $d$ is the inter-microphone spacing, and $\theta_{max}$ represents the maximum angle of arrival.

To ensure reliable operation in the 2–4 kHz band—critical for impulse discrimination—the spacing was fixed at $d = 4.2$ cm. Under worst-case angular arrival ($\theta_{max} = 90^\circ$):
\begin{equation}
f_{alias} = \frac{343}{2 \cdot 0.042} \approx 4083 \text{ Hz}
\end{equation}
This places the aliasing boundary safely above the 4 kHz operational ceiling. In practice, this margin prevents spatial ambiguity for the target impulse frequencies. The geometry is deliberately simple. While volumetric arrays provide elevation resolution, the linear configuration balances directional capability with deployability in corridor ceilings.

\subsection{Buffer Management and Latency Bounding}
Latency was treated as a primary design constraint throughout the implementation. The pipeline uses a lock-free circular ring buffer for PCM ingestion, avoiding blocking synchronization that introduces unpredictable delays.

Let:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $T_{frame} = 100ms$ represent the window length required to capture the transient decay of an impulsive event.
    \item $T_{proc} \approx 15–20ms$ represent processing time on the target ARM hardware.
\end{itemize}

Processing time decomposes into $T_{fft} \approx 10–15ms$ and $T_{gcc} \approx 5–10ms$. The total pipeline latency therefore satisfies:
\begin{equation}
T_{total} = T_{frame} + T_{proc} < 120ms
\end{equation}
This bound was measured empirically under single-stream load. By constraining latency explicitly, the architecture avoids queue accumulation and ensures near-immediate response for subsequent processing stages. Once spectral and periodicity features are extracted—and either accepted or rejected—the corresponding PCM segment is marked for overwrite. The acoustic module does not retain raw audio beyond the processing window.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
The processing pipeline was intentionally designed to remain computationally lightweight. For a frame of $N$ samples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RMS trigger computation operates in $O(N)$.
    \item Spectral gating requires an FFT with complexity $O(N \log N)$.
    \item GCC-PHAT localization requires two forward FFTs and one inverse FFT, also bounded by $O(N \log N)$.
\end{itemize}
Because no spatial grid search (e.g., SRP-PHAT) is performed, complexity does not scale with 3D search resolution. On the evaluated quad-core ARM device, total CPU utilization remained below 15\%, leaving headroom for concurrent node processes. The complexity profile was chosen deliberately to ensure scalability across dense institutional deployments.

\subsection{Anomaly Verification Routine (Spectral Gating)}
High-energy amplitude alone does not reliably indicate a safety anomaly. To distinguish impulsive broadband events from benign impacts, we compute a logarithmic discrimination index $\delta$ comparing energy in high- and low-frequency bands.

[Image showing acoustic spectral gating identifying a high-frequency impulsive anomaly against low-frequency background noise]

Prior to frequency transformation, each frame is windowed using a Hann function to reduce spectral leakage:
\begin{equation}
w(n) = 0.5 \left( 1 - \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right) \right)
\end{equation}

Let $Energy_{Low}$ represent magnitude-weighted energy in the 0–500 Hz band, and $Energy_{High}$ represent magnitude-weighted energy in the 2–4 kHz band. The discrimination index is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\delta = \log_{10}\left(\frac{Energy_{High}}{Energy_{Low}}\right)
\end{equation}
Because the ratio is logarithmic, amplitude scaling effects are normalized. Impulsive anomalies typically exhibit strong high-frequency harmonics, yielding:
\begin{equation}
\delta > 0.40
\end{equation}
This corresponds to approximately a 2.5× linear energy ratio. Mechanical impacts, by contrast, concentrate energy at lower frequencies and produce $\delta$ values near zero or negative.

Algorithm \ref{alg:verification} implements this two-stage verification: spectral gating followed by periodic rejection. The structure intentionally favors early rejection to conserve compute cycles before invoking autocorrelation. The filtering sequence proved essential in reducing false positives during corridor testing.

% --- ALGORITHM 1 ---
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Anomaly Verification Routine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $AudioFrame$ (N samples, Windowed)
\STATE \textbf{// Step 1: Spectral Gating}
\STATE $Spectrum \gets FFT(AudioFrame)$
\STATE $E_{Low} \gets \sum_{f=0}^{500} |Spectrum(f)|$ 
\STATE $E_{High} \gets \sum_{f=2000}^{4000} |Spectrum(f)|$ 
\STATE $\delta \gets \log_{10}(E_{High} / E_{Low})$
\IF{$\delta < 0.40$}
    \STATE \textbf{DISCARD\_LOW\_FREQ\_NOISE}
    \RETURN
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{// Step 2: Periodic Rejection}
\STATE $R_{xx} \gets \text{Autocorrelate}(AudioFrame)$
\STATE $Peak_{zero} \gets R_{xx}[0]$
\STATE $Peak_{max} \gets \max(R_{xx}[k_{min}:N-1])$
\STATE $\rho \gets Peak_{max} / Peak_{zero}$
\IF{$\rho > \rho_{thresh}$}
    \STATE \textbf{DISCARD\_PERIODIC\_NOISE}
\ELSE
    \STATE \textbf{CONFIRM\_CHAOTIC\_ANOMALY}
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:verification}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Physical Testbed Description}
Experimental evaluation was conducted in a corridor environment chosen specifically to replicate non-line-of-sight monitoring conditions. The corridor measured 2.4 m in width, 3.0 m in height, and 30 m in length, including an intersecting L-shaped junction.

Impulse response profiling yielded $RT_{60} \approx 1.2s$ and $D_c \approx 2.1m$. These measurements confirm that the environment lies well within the reverberant operating regime $\mathcal{R}_{rev}$ defined earlier. In other words, beyond approximately two meters, reverberant energy dominates the direct path. This condition stresses localization algorithms and serves as a meaningful validation scenario rather than a favorable acoustic case.

\subsection{Dataset Generation via RIR Convolution}
To avoid relying exclusively on live staged events, a hybrid dataset was constructed. First, the Room Impulse Response (RIR) of the corridor was measured using logarithmic sine sweeps emitted from a calibrated omnidirectional loudspeaker at multiple distances along the NLOS segment. The image-source method \cite{b30} was used to characterize reflection paths.

Next, dry anechoic samples from FSD50K (impulsive vocals) and MIVIA (glass break events) were convolved with the measured RIRs. This procedure embeds the true multipath structure of the corridor into otherwise clean samples, preserving realistic reverberation characteristics. Controlled SNR injection was then applied to evaluate performance degradation under varying ambient floors.

\subsection{Ablation Study on Heuristic Filters}
To isolate the contribution of each filtering stage, a 300-event mixed subset (150 true impulses, 150 mechanical/impact noises) was evaluated under incremental pipeline configurations.

The baseline SPL-only trigger yields a Recall of 99.5\% but a False Positive Rate of 42.6\%. This configuration is operationally unusable; benign door slams and HVAC events frequently exceed amplitude thresholds.

Introducing the logarithmic spectral gate ($\delta$) reduces FPR dramatically to 8.2\%, while Recall drops modestly to 96.0\%. This confirms that frequency discrimination eliminates many heavy impact artifacts. Adding periodic rejection ($\rho$) further reduces FPR to 1.8\%, with Recall at 94.5\% (Table \ref{tab:ablation}). The slight recall reduction reflects conservative filtering, but the false positive suppression is substantial. The ablation confirms that both heuristics are necessary. Either in isolation leaves the pipeline vulnerable to specific mechanical artifacts.

\section{Discussion}

The experimental results highlight several practical considerations for acoustic sensing in reverberant corridors.

\subsection{Acoustic Robustness and Filtering Trade-offs}
Periodic rejection ($\rho$) proved especially important during real corridor deployments. Early baseline trials relying on amplitude thresholds produced frequent nuisance alerts. Layered filtering helped stabilize the alert stream without introducing semantic inference complexity.

Unlike transformer-based AED systems, this pipeline intentionally avoids high-level categorization. The trade-off is deliberate. In response scenarios, latency and spatial directionality matter more than exact event labeling. The pipeline intentionally sacrifices semantic richness to preserve deterministic response time.

\subsection{Reverberation and GCC-PHAT Behavior}
Within $\mathcal{R}_{rev}$, standard cross-correlation becomes unreliable due to echo dominance. PHAT weighting mitigates this by equalizing spectral magnitude, sharpening the delay peak associated with the direct path.

In practice, performance remained stable across corridor positions, with occasional angular deviations in highly metallic segments. A known limitation lies in the linear array geometry. The current implementation estimates azimuth only. Multi-level stairwells or open atriums would require tetrahedral or spherical arrays to recover elevation components accurately. The present configuration is primarily optimized for planar corridor deployment.

\subsection{Privacy by Architectural Constraint}
Continuous acoustic sensing raises legitimate privacy concerns \cite{b4, b33}. The pipeline addresses this through processing restrictions rather than policy alone.

Raw PCM audio is processed within a bounded RAM buffer and overwritten immediately after feature extraction. No speech-to-text or semantic modeling is performed. The only transmitted output is a lightweight metadata tuple, such as:
\begin{equation}
[\texttt{TIMESTAMP, ANOMALY\_CONFIDENCE, AZIMUTH\_ANGLE}]
\end{equation}

This data minimization strategy aligns with “Privacy by Design” principles \cite{b32}. The acoustic module cannot reconstruct conversations because it never retains them. Scalability follows naturally from this constraint: metadata payloads are lightweight, allowing hundreds of nodes to operate on standard IoT backhaul networks without saturating bandwidth.

% ==========================================
% VIII. CONCLUSION
% ==========================================
\section{Conclusion}
This study addressed a practical limitation of camera-based perception: the presence of visually occluded regions within indoor environments. By combining spectral discrimination ($\delta$), periodic rejection ($\rho$), and GCC-PHAT localization with explicit latency bounds, the acoustic sensing module extends spatial awareness into non-line-of-sight spaces.

Within the evaluated 15-meter safety envelope, detection rates exceeded approximately 95\% under controlled conditions. Layered filtering reduced false positives to roughly 1.8\%, while end-to-end processing latency remained below about 120 ms. Raw audio samples are overwritten immediately after feature extraction, limiting retained information to lightweight directional metadata.

The results indicate that lightweight signal-processing pipelines can extend safety monitoring into non-line-of-sight spaces without requiring persistent audio storage or high-latency semantic analysis.
